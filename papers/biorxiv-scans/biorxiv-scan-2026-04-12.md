## Moderately relevant papers

### 1. A multiplexed striatal architecture for generalized spatial goal progress

Takakuwa N, Golipour Z, Ito HT. 2026.
"A multiplexed striatal architecture for generalized spatial goal progress." bioRxiv.
https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.04.08.716650v1

**Findings:** Large-scale population recordings and causal perturbations in rats
navigating toward dynamically changing goals in mazes of varying geometry. The nucleus
accumbens (NAc) encodes a scale-invariant distance-to-goal signal normalised by total
journey length. This signal transfers to novel journeys and tracks physical path length
rather than remaining time. A decoder trained on this NAc signal generalises across
arbitrary start–goal combinations, identifying NAc as a locus for abstract spatial
progress computation. A multiplexed architecture was identified where different cell
populations encode different components of the spatial progress signal.

**Relevance to hm2p:** Indirectly relevant as a study of spatial coding in a freely
moving rat maze task. The rose maze provides a natural start-goal structure (central
hub to arms), and a distance-to-goal or distance-from-centre signal might be present
in RSP Penk+ or non-Penk populations. The multiplexed population architecture is a
useful conceptual parallel: our two RSP populations may similarly multiplex spatial and
HD information. The NAc result also constrains interpretation — if RSP HD cells are
influenced by goal-directed state, we should check whether HD tuning varies as a
function of distance from the maze centre. Not a central comparison point but worth
noting as context for population-level analyses.

### 2. Mapping projectome heterogeneity of subiculum neuron cell types

Saustad AW, Bienkowski MS. 2026.
"Mapping Projectome Heterogeneity of Subiculum Neuron Cell Types." bioRxiv.
https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.04.01.716004v1

**Findings:** Virtual tract-tracing using the Digital Brain Mouse Projectome Atlas was
used to assess how connectivity of 689 subiculum projection neurons aligns with
genetically defined cell types (via the mouse Hippocampus Gene Expression Atlas, HGEA).
The subiculum was classified into 12 HGEA cell-type groups based on laminar and columnar
distributions. Connectivity patterns corresponded to distinct brain-wide networks
involved in spatial navigation, social behaviour, and neuroendocrine regulation.
Subiculum layers and columns show markedly different projection targets, including
differential input to cortical regions such as RSP.

**Note:** Posted April 1, 2026, which is at the edge of the 7-day window. Included
here because it was not covered in the April 6 scan.

**Relevance to hm2p:** The subiculum is a major hippocampal output to RSP and a
significant source of spatial and directional input to the retrosplenial network. The
finding that distinct genetically defined subiculum cell types project to different
cortical targets means that RSP Penk+ and non-Penk populations may receive spatially
segregated input from distinct subiculum populations, not a uniform hippocampal drive.
This is relevant for interpreting cell-type-specific differences in HD tuning: if a
particular subiculum population targets Penk+ layers preferentially, this could explain
differential spatial information content. The genetic identity of RSP inputs is largely
unexplored, and this paper provides a resource (Digital Brain Mouse Projectome Atlas)
for investigating which subiculum cell types project to granular vs dysgranular RSP.
Background for the discussion only at this stage.

## Tangentially relevant / methods papers

### 3. Three-photon microscopy of mouse brain structure and function at 2 mm depth and beyond

Rahman M, Liu C, Ouzounov DG, Xu C. 2026.
"Three photon microscopy of mouse brain structure and function at 2 mm depth and
beyond." bioRxiv.
https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.04.06.716666v1

**Findings:** Improved 1300-nm three-photon microscope for maximal excitation and
collection efficiency enables structural imaging to 2.5 mm depth and functional
calcium imaging to 2 mm depth in the intact mouse brain. The system achieves
cellular-resolution recording at depths previously inaccessible to two-photon
microscopy. Demonstrated in mouse cortex and subcortical structures through the
intact skull.

**Relevance to hm2p:** Technology paper providing context for deep-brain imaging
capabilities. Our current head-mounted two-photon system images a single cortical
plane; the 2-mm depth capability of three-photon microscopy would, in principle,
allow imaging subcortical HD circuit components (ADN thalamus is at ~3 mm depth
in mice, so not yet reachable). Relevant for the future directions section only.
No impact on current analysis.
